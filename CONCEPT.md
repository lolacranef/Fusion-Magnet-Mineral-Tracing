# Coilchain: Magnet Mineral Rule Gaps

*Which laws reach the mining regions behind fusion superconducting magnets, and what each gap lets through, in plain terms. Rule terms checked 9 November 2026. Research, not legal advice.*

Interactive site: <https://lolacranef.github.io/Fusion-Magnet-Mineral-Tracing/>. Methods and every source: [DATA_METHODS_SOURCES.md](DATA_METHODS_SOURCES.md).

## Who this is for

### Primary: policy reviewers and public fusion funders
- **Who:** staff reviewing the EU Conflict Minerals Regulation and Japan's due diligence guidelines, officials building the EU forced labor risk database, and program officers who fund fusion through DOE milestone awards, UK STEP and national research ministries.
- **Decision they make:** which minerals and regions a rule covers, and what conditions come with public money.
- **Why now:** the EU regulation is under review in 2026, Japan's review ends in 2026, and the EU forced labor ban starts in December 2027. Fusion buys tiny volumes and much of it is publicly funded, so a sourcing condition costs little and reaches nearly every magnet maker.
- **Use it to:** show, region by region, where a mineral falls between laws, and in plain terms what that gap lets happen.

### Secondary: investigators, NGOs and journalists
- **Decision:** which tool to use against a documented abuse, such as a §307 allegation to CBP or a sanctions referral.
- **Use it to:** match a region's evidence to the one rule that actually applies today, using the plain-language rule guide and links to the official texts.

### Secondary: compliance and procurement at fusion companies
- **Decision:** what to ask their tape and wire suppliers, beyond what the law requires.
- **Use it to:** see which risks no law will flag for them, and build those questions into supplier contracts.

## Verdict key

| Verdict | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Applies | The rule covers this region and mineral today |
| Partly reaches | Covers part of the problem, or only in some cases |
| Not yet in force | Would cover it, but not until a future date |
| Voluntary only | Recommends action but creates no legal duty |
| Doesn't reach | Out of scope by mineral, geography or type of harm |
| Not relevant | No connection to this region |

## The rules

### Conflict-mineral rules

#### Dodd-Frank §1502 (US)

A US securities law that makes listed companies say whether their products contain tin, tantalum, tungsten or gold from Congo or its neighbors. It bans nothing: the pressure comes from public disclosure.

- **Minerals covered:** Tin, tantalum, tungsten, gold. Not niobium or rare earths.
- **Where:** DRC and 9 neighbors
- **Who must act:** US-listed companies
- **What it requires:** Country-of-origin inquiry, disclosed on Form SD. No ban.
- **Status:** In force; detailed report unenforced since 2017; future uncertain.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Conflict minerals (3TG):* Cassiterite, coltan, wolframite and gold, and their derivatives tin, tantalum and tungsten. Niobium, which also comes from coltan, is not a listed derivative.
  - *Covered countries:* The DRC and the nine countries that border it.
  - *Reasonable country of origin inquiry:* A good-faith check, usually a supplier survey, on where the minerals came from. Every filer must do one.
  - *Form SD:* The annual 'specialized disclosure' filed with the SEC, due by 31 May each year, or the next business day if that falls on a weekend.
  - *Conflict Minerals Report:* A fuller due diligence report for minerals that may come from covered countries. Since an April 2017 statement by SEC staff, companies that skip it have not faced enforcement.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Search SEC EDGAR for form type SD to see which smelters and refiners a company names, and whether it filed a Conflict Minerals Report.
  - Compare a company's smelter list with the Responsible Minerals Initiative's lists of audited smelters.
- **Official text and tools:** [15 U.S.C. §78m(p): the statute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/78m) · [17 CFR §240.13p-1: the SEC rule](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.13p-1) · [SEC EDGAR filing search](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/) · [Responsible Minerals Initiative smelter lists](https://www.responsiblemineralsinitiative.org/smelters-refiners-lists/)
- **Secondary sources:** [Skadden (May 2026)](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/05/conflict-minerals-disclosures-due-june-1-2026) · [Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/viewpoints/102kbie/is-u-s-conflict-minerals-disclosure-nearing-an-end) · [Claigan FAQ](https://www.claigan.com/conflict-minerals-faq/)

#### EU Conflict Minerals Regulation (EU 2017/821)

An EU regulation that makes companies importing tin, tantalum, tungsten or gold ores and metals into the EU check their supply chains against the OECD Guidance. It covers conflict areas anywhere, but only those four metals and only as ores or metals.

- **Minerals covered:** Tin, tantalum, tungsten, gold: ores and metals only.
- **Where:** Conflict-affected and high-risk areas worldwide
- **Who must act:** EU importers of ores and metals above thresholds
- **What it requires:** OECD-based supply chain due diligence.
- **Status:** Applies since January 2021.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Union importer:* The company that declares the ores or metals for release into the EU. Importers of finished goods, such as wire or magnets, are not covered.
  - *CAHRA:* Conflict-affected and high-risk areas: places in armed conflict, fragile after conflict, or with weak governance and widespread abuses. The EU publishes an indicative, non-binding list; importers must still judge risk themselves.
  - *Annex I thresholds:* Minimum yearly import volumes. Importers below them have no duties.
  - *Competent authority:* The national body in each EU country that checks importers and can order corrective action. Each country sets its own rules for penalties.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Ask an EU country's competent authority whether a named importer has been checked.
  - Use the CAHRA definition to argue that an area such as Wa State should trigger enhanced checks for tin.
- **Official text and tools:** [Regulation (EU) 2017/821 (EUR-Lex)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/821/oj)
- **Secondary sources:** [Ropes & Gray on the CAHRA list (Dec 2020)](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2020/12/conflict-minerals-compliance-european-commission-publishes-global-list-of-conflict-affected) · [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/the-eu-new-mandatory-supply-chain-diligence-rules-batteries-raw-materials/)

#### EU Battery Regulation due diligence (EU 2023/1542)

An EU regulation on batteries that includes supply chain due diligence for four battery minerals. It is the EU's most mineral-specific human rights rule, but rare earths, tin and niobium are not in it.

- **Minerals covered:** Cobalt, natural graphite, lithium, nickel only.
- **Where:** Global
- **Who must act:** Companies placing batteries on the EU market
- **What it requires:** Raw-material due diligence.
- **Status:** Postponed to 18 August 2027.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Raw materials in scope:* Cobalt, natural graphite, lithium and nickel only.
  - *Due diligence policy:* Companies placing batteries on the EU market must check their raw material supply chains and have their policy verified by an independent body.
  - *Postponement:* The duties were delayed to 18 August 2027, and a proposal would limit them to companies above €150m turnover.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Mainly useful as a model: it shows the EU can write mineral-specific duties beyond tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold.
- **Official text and tools:** [Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 (EUR-Lex)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1542/oj)
- **Secondary sources:** [A&L Goodbody](https://www.algoodbody.com/insights-publications/batteries-due-diligence-a-two-year-pause-and-reduced-scope) · [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/the-eu-new-mandatory-supply-chain-diligence-rules-batteries-raw-materials/)

### Forced-labor bans

#### EU Forced Labor Regulation (EU 2024/3015)

An EU regulation that will ban any product made wholly or partly with forced labor from being sold in, or exported from, the EU. It covers every stage, including the mine, but only forced labor, not pollution or conflict financing.

- **Minerals covered:** All products.
- **Where:** Any origin, including extraction
- **Who must act:** Anyone placing products on the EU market
- **What it requires:** Ban on products made with forced labor.
- **Status:** Applies from 14 December 2027.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Forced labor:* As defined in ILO Convention No. 29: work extracted under threat of a penalty that a person has not offered voluntarily. Forced child labor is included.
  - *Lead competent authority:* The European Commission handles cases about risks outside the EU; national authorities handle cases inside it.
  - *Risk-based approach:* Authorities focus on the products, sectors and areas with the highest risk, using a public database the Commission is building.
  - *Burden of proof:* Authorities must establish that forced labor was used, but a company's refusal to cooperate can count against it.
  - *Ban decision:* Orders the product off the EU market and blocked at the border, and requires it to be donated, recycled or destroyed.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Once the EU's submission portal opens, anyone can send evidence about a product or area.
  - Detailed first-hand testimony, like the Shan Human Rights Foundation's, is the kind of evidence the regulation is built to use.
- **Official text and tools:** [Regulation (EU) 2024/3015 (EUR-Lex)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/3015/oj)
- **Secondary sources:** [Mayer Brown (Jun 2026)](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/the-eu-forced-labour-regulation-10-questions-every-general-counsel-should-be-asking)

#### Tariff Act §307 import ban (US)

A 1930 US trade law that bans imports of goods made wholly or partly by forced, convict or indentured labor, including forced child labor, from any country. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) enforces it.

- **Minerals covered:** Any forced-labor goods.
- **Where:** Any country
- **Who must act:** US importers; anyone can petition CBP
- **What it requires:** CBP can detain on reasonable but not conclusive evidence.
- **Status:** In force.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Withhold Release Order (WRO):* CBP can hold shipments at the border when information reasonably, but not conclusively, indicates forced labor.
  - *Finding:* A stronger CBP determination that goods are made with forced labor, which allows them to be seized.
  - *Allegation:* Any person outside CBP who has reason to believe goods are made with forced labor may tell CBP. NGOs and journalists regularly do.
  - *Consumptive demand exception:* An old loophole that let in forced-labor goods if US demand could not otherwise be met. Congress removed it in 2016.
  - *Importer's response:* When goods are held, the importer has three months to show it made every reasonable effort to trace the labor.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Submit an allegation through CBP's e-Allegations portal, naming the product, the producer and the evidence.
  - Check CBP's public list of Withhold Release Orders and Findings for past cases and precedents.
- **Official text and tools:** [19 U.S.C. §1307: the statute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1307) · [19 CFR §12.42: CBP's procedure](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/12.42) · [CBP: Withhold Release Orders and Findings](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/forced-labor/withhold-release-orders-and-findings) · [CBP e-Allegations portal](https://eallegations.cbp.gov/)
- **Secondary sources:** [CRS IF11360 (Jul 2026)](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-07-09_IF11360_c9e63009c902a08f9494a2def20f5e391c71b6fa.html)

#### Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (US)

A 2021 US law that presumes goods from China's Xinjiang region, or from companies on a government list, are made with forced labor. Importers must prove otherwise, which reverses the usual burden of proof.

- **Minerals covered:** Any goods.
- **Where:** Xinjiang and Entity List companies
- **Who must act:** US importers
- **What it requires:** Presumption of forced labor; importer must rebut.
- **Status:** In force; 187 listed entities after Aug 2026.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Rebuttable presumption:* Goods are assumed to be made with forced labor unless the importer proves they are not.
  - *Clear and convincing evidence:* A high standard of proof, above 'more likely than not', that importers must meet to overturn the presumption.
  - *UFLPA Entity List:* Companies named by an interagency task force led by the Department of Homeland Security. Their goods fall under the presumption wherever they are made. 187 entities after August 2026.
  - *Priority sectors:* Sectors flagged for enforcement, including aluminum, copper, lithium and steel. Rare earths are not named.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Check the DHS UFLPA Entity List for any processor or trader in a supply chain.
  - Evidence that Myanmar ore is processed by a listed company would bring it under the presumption.
- **Official text and tools:** [Public Law 117-78 (congress.gov)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/6256) · [DHS: UFLPA Entity List](https://www.dhs.gov/uflpa-entity-list) · [CBP: UFLPA guidance](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/forced-labor/UFLPA)
- **Secondary sources:** [Troutman (Aug 2026)](https://www.troutman.com/insights/dhs-adds-43-companies-to-uflpa-entity-list-in-largest-ever-single-expansion-effective-august-3/)

### Due diligence laws

#### Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (EU 2026/470)

An EU directive requiring the largest companies to find and address human rights and environmental harm in their own operations and their chain of activities. A 2026 amendment sharply cut its scope and delayed it to 2029.

- **Minerals covered:** All (harm-based).
- **Where:** Whole chain of activities, worldwide
- **Who must act:** EU companies with 5,000+ staff and over €1.5bn turnover; non-EU companies with over €1.5bn EU turnover
- **What it requires:** Risk-based due diligence.
- **Status:** Comply by 26 July 2029.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Directive:* EU law that each member country must turn into national law ('transposition') before companies are bound by it.
  - *Chain of activities:* A company's upstream suppliers, direct and indirect, and some downstream partners. Mines are included in principle.
  - *Adverse impact:* A harm to people or the environment listed in the directive's annex, including forced and child labor.
  - *Risk-based scoping:* Since the 2026 amendment, companies identify where harm is most likely and severe and look deeper there, instead of mapping every supplier.
  - *Penalties:* Fines capped at 3% of worldwide turnover. The amendment removed the EU-wide civil liability rule.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Check whether a supplier's parent group meets the thresholds: 5,000+ staff and over €1.5bn turnover, or over €1.5bn EU turnover for non-EU companies.
  - From 2029, raise documented harm with the company and with the national supervisory authority.
- **Official text and tools:** [Directive (EU) 2024/1760, current version (EUR-Lex)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/1760/oj) · [Amending Directive (EU) 2026/470 (EUR-Lex)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2026/470/oj/eng)
- **Secondary sources:** [Covington (Feb 2026)](https://www.cov.com/news-and-insights/insights/2026/02/eu-csddd-csrd-omnibus-published-in-official-journal-transposition-delegated-acts-and-guidelines-are-next)

#### Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (LkSG) (Germany)

A German law requiring companies with 1,000+ staff in Germany to manage human rights and environmental risks in their supply chains. Duties are strongest for their own operations and direct suppliers. It is being cut back.

- **Minerals covered:** All (harm-based).
- **Where:** Global, usually direct suppliers only
- **Who must act:** Companies in Germany with 1,000+ staff
- **What it requires:** Risk analysis, prevention, complaints procedure.
- **Status:** Being cut back; amendment in Bundestag.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Direct supplier:* A company the buyer contracts with directly. Risk analysis is required for these.
  - *Indirect supplier:* Suppliers further up the chain, such as smelters and mines. Duties apply only when the company has 'substantiated knowledge' of a possible violation.
  - *Substantiated knowledge:* Concrete, verifiable information pointing to a possible violation, such as an NGO report or a complaint.
  - *Complaints procedure:* Every covered company must run one, open to anyone, including people affected at indirect suppliers.
  - *BAFA:* The Federal Office for Economic Affairs and Export Control, which enforces the law and accepts complaints.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Send documented evidence about a mine to a German buyer's complaints procedure. This can create 'substantiated knowledge' and trigger duties toward indirect suppliers.
  - Complain to BAFA if the company does not respond.
- **Official text and tools:** [LkSG, official text in German](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/lksg/) · [BAFA: overview in English](https://www.bafa.de/EN/Supply_Chain_Act/Overview/overview_node.html) · [BAFA: complaints procedure](https://www.bafa.de/EN/Supply_Chain_Act/Complaints_Procedure/complaints_procedure_node.html)
- **Secondary sources:** [Taylor Wessing (Jan 2026)](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2026/01/lksg)

#### Human Rights Due Diligence Guidelines (Japan)

Japanese government guidelines, published in September 2022, that ask companies doing business in Japan to carry out human rights due diligence across their supply chains. They are voluntary.

- **Minerals covered:** All.
- **Where:** Global value chains
- **Who must act:** Companies operating in Japan
- **What it requires:** UNGP-aligned due diligence, recommended.
- **Status:** Voluntary; review ends 2026.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Human rights due diligence:* A repeated process to identify, prevent and reduce harm to people, track the results and report on them.
  - *UN Guiding Principles:* The 2011 UN framework stating that companies have a responsibility to respect human rights. The guidelines are based on it.
  - *Grievance mechanism:* A channel through which affected people can raise concerns with a company.
  - *Voluntary:* There is no penalty for ignoring the guidelines. A government review is due by the end of 2026, with any bill in 2027.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Ask Japanese tape and wire makers whether they apply the guidelines to rare earths and tin, and to name their separators and smelters.
- **Official text and tools:** [Guidelines, provisional English translation (METI)](https://www.meti.go.jp/policy/economy/business-jinken/guidelines/provisionalenglishtranslation.pdf) · [METI: business and human rights](https://www.meti.go.jp/english/policy/economy/biz_human_rights/index.html)
- **Secondary sources:** [METI: business and human rights](https://www.meti.go.jp/english/policy/economy/biz_human_rights/index.html)

#### OECD Minerals Due Diligence Guidance (International)

The international reference standard for responsible mineral sourcing, adopted by OECD governments. It applies to all minerals from all conflict-affected and high-risk areas, but is voluntary unless a law adopts it.

- **Minerals covered:** All minerals.
- **Where:** All areas
- **Who must act:** Any company in a mineral supply chain
- **What it requires:** Five-step framework.
- **Status:** Voluntary reference standard.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Five-step framework:* 1. Build management systems. 2. Identify and assess risks. 3. Respond to them. 4. Have smelters and refiners independently audited. 5. Report publicly every year.
  - *Annex II risks:* Risks a company must never contribute to: torture, forced labor, the worst forms of child labor and war crimes; support to armed groups; abusive security forces; bribery and money laundering.
  - *Upstream and downstream:* Upstream runs from the mine to the smelter or refiner; downstream from the smelter to the final product. Smelters are the 'choke point' where origin can still be checked.
  - *Third edition (2016):* Clarified that the Guidance covers all minerals, not only tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Use Annex II as a checklist when reporting on a mine: each documented abuse maps onto a named risk.
  - Complaints about multinational companies can go to OECD National Contact Points under the related OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises.
- **Official text and tools:** [OECD Guidance: official page](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-due-diligence-guidance-for-responsible-supply-chains-of-minerals-from-conflict-affected-and-high-risk-areas_9789264252479-en.html) · [Full text (PDF)](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2016/04/oecd-due-diligence-guidance-for-responsible-supply-chains-of-minerals-from-conflict-affected-and-high-risk-areas_g1g65996/9789264252479-en.pdf)
- **Secondary sources:** [International Tin Association](https://www.internationaltin.org/oecd/)

### Other levers

#### Targeted sanctions (US / EU)

US and EU sanctions freeze the assets of named people and companies and bar others from dealing with them. They target who is involved, not which mineral is traded.

- **Minerals covered:** Not mineral-based.
- **Where:** Country programs
- **Who must act:** Anyone dealing with listed parties
- **What it requires:** No dealings; assets frozen.
- **Status:** Varies by region.
- **Key terms:**
  - *SDN list:* The US Treasury's Specially Designated Nationals list. US persons may not deal with anyone on it.
  - *Asset freeze:* Funds and property of a listed party are blocked.
  - *50% rule:* Under US rules, a company owned 50% or more by listed parties is treated as blocked even if it is not named.
  - *Travel ban:* EU measures can also bar listed people from entering the EU.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Search OFAC's Sanctions List Search and the EU Sanctions Map for any trader, exporter or refiner named in reporting.
  - Documented links between a buyer and a listed armed group can support a referral to US or EU authorities.
- **Official text and tools:** [OFAC: DRC-related sanctions](https://ofac.treasury.gov/sanctions-programs-and-country-information/democratic-republic-of-the-congo-related-sanctions) · [OFAC Sanctions List Search](https://sanctionssearch.ofac.treas.gov/) · [EU Sanctions Map](https://www.sanctionsmap.eu/)
- **Secondary sources:** [The Star (Apr 2026)](https://www.thestar.com.my/news/world/2026/04/27/eu-extends-sanctions-against-myanmar-by-a-year) · [ThePrint (Jul 2025)](https://theprint.in/world/us-lifts-some-myanmar-sanctions-after-general-asked-trump-for-relief/2702840) · [Mining.com (Aug 2025)](https://www.mining.com/web/us-sanctions-armed-group-mining-firms-over-illicit-congo-minerals) · [AllAfrica / Global Witness (Apr 2025)](https://allafrica.com/stories/202504280598.html)

#### Rare earth export controls (China)

Chinese export rules that require a government license for every export of seven medium and heavy rare earths, including yttrium and gadolinium. The aim is strategic control, not human rights.

- **Minerals covered:** Seven medium and heavy rare earths, including yttrium and gadolinium.
- **Where:** Exports from China
- **Who must act:** Chinese exporters
- **What it requires:** Per-shipment license; strategic test only.
- **Status:** Fully enforced since April 2025.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Announcement No. 18 (April 2025):* Added samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium, and their compounds, alloys and magnets, to China's dual-use export control list. Still in force.
  - *Dual-use item:* Goods with both civilian and military uses, which need a license to export under China's Export Control Law.
  - *Announcement No. 61 (October 2025):* Extended controls to products made outside China containing as little as 0.1% Chinese-origin rare earths by value. China suspended it on 7 November 2025 until 10 November 2026. Check whether the suspension has been extended.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - License data are not public, so the controls hide origin rather than reveal it. Ask importers for the separator's name and the export license reference.
- **Official text and tools:** [IEA summary of Announcement No. 18](https://www.iea.org/policies/26796-export-controls-on-certain-medium-and-heavy-rare-earth-items) · [CSET translation of Announcement No. 61](https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/mofcom-notice-2025-61) · [Pillsbury: the November 2025 suspension](https://www.pillsburylaw.com/en/news-and-insights/china-suspends-export-controls-certain-critical-minerals-related-items.html)
- **Secondary sources:** [Holland & Knight on Announcement No. 18 (Apr 2025)](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/04/china-imposes-export-controls-on-medium-and-heavy-rare-earth-materials) · [Pillsbury on the November 2025 suspension](https://www.pillsburylaw.com/en/news-and-insights/china-suspends-export-controls-certain-critical-minerals-related-items.html)

#### Producer-country enforcement (Local)

Each producing country's own mining, labor and environmental laws. On paper they ban much of the documented harm; in practice they work only where the state is in control.

- **Minerals covered:** Whatever is mined there.
- **Where:** Within the country
- **Who must act:** Miners, traders, officials
- **What it requires:** National mining law.
- **Status:** Varies by region.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Myanmar:* Foreign investment in small and medium-scale mineral production is illegal and no exemptions have been issued, yet mining continues under militia permits. Myanmar was suspended from the EITI after the 2021 coup.
  - *Indonesia:* Courts convicted those behind illegal tin trading, and a 2025 crackdown targeted 1,000 illegal mines. Indonesia is an EITI member.
  - *DR Congo:* State law does not operate in M23-held Rubaya, where the group runs a parallel administration that taxes minerals. The DRC is an EITI member.
  - *EITI:* The Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative: a global standard under which member governments publish mining licenses, payments and ownership data.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - EITI country pages and reports show who holds mining licenses and what they paid.
  - Court records in Indonesia's tin case name the companies and smelters involved.
- **Official text and tools:** [EITI: Myanmar](https://eiti.org/countries/myanmar) · [EITI: Indonesia](https://eiti.org/countries/indonesia) · [EITI: DR Congo](https://eiti.org/countries/democratic-republic-congo)
- **Secondary sources:** [Mongabay on Global Witness](https://news.mongabay.com/2022/08/toxic-rare-earth-mines-fuel-deforestation-rights-abuses-in-myanmar-report-says/) · [The Irrawaddy on Wa tin](https://www.irrawaddy.com/?p=127037) · [VOI (Jul 2025)](https://voi.id/en/amp/491796) · [Antara (Sep 2025)](https://en.antaranews.com/news/383205/govt-calls-for-shutdown-of-1000-illegal-tin-mines-in-bangka-belitung) · [Reuters via Monitor](https://www.monitor.co.ug/uganda/news/inside-the-mine-that-feeds-the-tech-world-and-funds-congo-s-rebels-5154474)

## Region: Kachin, Myanmar

*Yttrium · heavy rare earths*

**Route:** Kachin mines (Myanmar) → Trucked to Yunnan (border crossing) → Separation (China) → REBCO tape (fusion magnets)

**Documented harm**

- **2,700+** rare earth mining pools by March 2022. Satellite images commissioned by Global Witness in March 2022 found more than 2,700 rare earth collection pools at almost 300 locations in Kachin Special Region 1, spread over an area the size of Singapore. The mining is illegal under Myanmar law. By 2023 the number of mining sites had grown by more than 40%, to over 300. Sources: [Global Witness, Myanmar's poisoned mountains (Aug 2022)](https://globalwitness.org/en/campaigns/transition-minerals/myanmars-poisoned-mountains) · [Global Witness, Fuelling the future, poisoning the present (May 2024)](https://globalwitness.org/en/campaigns/transition-minerals/fuelling-the-future-poisoning-the-present-myanmars-rare-earth-boom)
- **~½** of world heavy rare earth output came from sites seized in 2024. In October 2024 the Kachin Independence Army took the Pangwa mining hub from a junta-aligned militia. An analyst at the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) estimates the seized sites produced about half of the world's heavy rare earths. Sources: [CNBC, quoting CSIS (Jun 2025)](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/06/24/chinas-rare-earth-dominance-myanmar-plays-a-critical-role-.html) · [ISP-Myanmar, Rare earth mining in Myanmar's war-torn regions (Jun 2025)](https://ispmyanmar.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Rare-Earth-Mining-in-Myanmars-War-Torn-Regions.pdf)
- **93%** of US yttrium compound imports came from China, 2020–23. The US imports all the yttrium it uses. The US Geological Survey reports that 93% of yttrium compound imports came from China in 2020–23, nearly all made from concentrates processed there, and notes that yttrium is also produced from clay deposits in Myanmar. Sources: [USGS, Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025: Yttrium](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2025/mcs2025-yttrium.pdf)
- **451** rare earth mines in Kachin's Irrawaddy basin; 260 opened since the 2021 coup. Stimson Center satellite analysis identified 451 rare earth mines in Kachin's part of the Irrawaddy basin over the past decade, 260 of them opened after the coup. Residents fear hazardous waste flows into the N'Mai Kha, an Irrawaddy tributary; the Irrawaddy basin is home to two-thirds of Myanmar's people. Sources: [Mongabay, reporting Stimson Center satellite data (Oct 2025)](https://news.mongabay.com/2025/10/more-thai-rivers-and-downstream-communities-at-risk-from-myanmars-rare-earth-mines) · [Global Witness, Myanmar's poisoned mountains (Aug 2022)](https://globalwitness.org/en/campaigns/transition-minerals/myanmars-poisoned-mountains)

**Coverage tally:** 5 partly reaches, 1 not yet in force, 2 voluntary only, 5 doesn't reach

| Rule | Verdict | What it says here | What the gap lets through |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dodd-Frank §1502 | Doesn't reach | Rare earths aren't on the list, and Myanmar isn't a covered country. | A US company can buy yttrium-based tape without ever asking where the yttrium was dug up. |
| EU Conflict Minerals Regulation | Doesn't reach | It reaches conflict areas anywhere, so Kachin qualifies on its own principles. But only tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold count. | Myanmar heavy rare earths enter the EU with no due diligence duty, while tin from the same conflict would need it. |
| EU Battery Regulation due diligence | Doesn't reach | It covers only cobalt, graphite, lithium and nickel. | Rare earths in magnets and superconductors sit outside even the EU's most mineral-specific law. |
| EU Forced Labor Regulation | Partly reaches | From December 2027 it bans products with forced labor at any stage. | Most documented harm in Kachin is pollution, illegal mining and armed-group revenue. None of that is forced labor, so the ban can't touch it. |
| Tariff Act §307 import ban | Partly reaches | It bans forced-labor goods from any country. | Same limit as the EU ban: environmental destruction and conflict financing are not forced labor. |
| Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act | Doesn't reach | The presumption applies only to Xinjiang and listed companies. | The US's strongest import tool flips the burden of proof only for Xinjiang. Myanmar ore gets no presumption. |
| Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive | Not yet in force | It would cover pollution and conflict financing in the chain, but only from July 2029 and only for very large companies. | Fusion start-ups and tape makers are far below 5,000 staff and €1.5bn turnover, so the companies closest to the risk are out of scope. |

| OECD Minerals Due Diligence Guidance | Voluntary only | The Guidance asks for due diligence on all minerals in all areas. | It is the right standard, but nobody is obliged to apply it to rare earths. |
| Targeted sanctions | Partly reaches | EU: asset freezes, travel bans and an arms embargo on 105 people and 22 entities, extended to May 2027. The US removed four junta-linked parties from its list in July 2025. | No measure targets the rare earth trade itself, so money from the mines can move without touching a listed name. |
| Rare earth export controls | Partly reaches | China licenses every export of yttrium and gadolinium. | The license is about leverage, not abuses. Once Myanmar ore is separated in China it leaves as controlled Chinese material, and its origin disappears. |
| Producer-country enforcement | Doesn't reach | Global Witness found the mines illegal under Myanmar law. | After the 2021 coup the rule of law collapsed, so illegality on paper has no effect on the ground. |

## Region: Shan & Wa, Myanmar

*Rare earths · tin*

**Route:** Wa & Shan mines (Myanmar) → Across the border (into China) → Separation & smelting (China) → REBCO tape · Nb₃Sn wire (fusion magnets)

**Documented harm**

- **Forced** labor by trafficked workers at a rare earth mine (May 2026). The Shan Human Rights Foundation published testimony from a trafficking survivor: Burmese scam-center workers were handed by regime police to United Wa State Army soldiers and forced to dig at a Chinese-operated rare earth mine in southern Mong Hsat, near the Thai border, kept apart from the regular workforce. Sources: [Shan Human Rights Foundation (May 2026)](https://shanhumanrights.org?p=5051) · [The Irrawaddy, via Business & Human Rights Resource Centre (May 2026)](https://www.business-humanrights.org/my/%E1%80%9E%E1%80%90%E1%80%84/myanmar-uwsa-alleged-of-trafficking-scam-workers-into-chinese-operated-rare-earth-mines)
- **63** rare earth mining sites near the Chinese border, most opened after the 2021 coup. Satellite analysis by the Shan Human Rights Foundation and the Stimson Center found 63 rare earth mining sites in Shan State near the Chinese border and two near Thailand. Some date from 2015, but most emerged after the coup. Sources: [Yale Environment 360](https://e360.yale.edu/features/myanmar-rare-earth-mining)
- **$40M** in losses downstream in Thailand from polluted rivers. Runoff from unregulated rare earth mines in Shan and Kachin states has polluted rivers flowing into northern Thailand, including the Kok, causing about $40 million in losses to farming, fishing and tourism. Of 58 mines along the Kok, Ruak and Lwe rivers, 44 opened between 2022 and 2025. Sources: [Mongabay, reporting Stimson Center satellite data (Oct 2025)](https://news.mongabay.com/2025/10/more-thai-rivers-and-downstream-communities-at-risk-from-myanmars-rare-earth-mines)

**Coverage tally:** 1 applies, 3 partly reaches, 2 not yet in force, 2 voluntary only, 5 doesn't reach

| Rule | Verdict | What it says here | What the gap lets through |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dodd-Frank §1502 | Doesn't reach | Tin counts, but only from the DRC region. | Wa tin can end up in US products with no disclosure at all. |
| EU Conflict Minerals Regulation | Partly reaches | EU importers of tin ores and metals must do due diligence on conflict areas anywhere, which should include Wa State. | It stops at the importer of raw tin. Wire and magnets made from it aren't covered, and rare earths from forced-labor mines are outside entirely. |
| EU Battery Regulation due diligence | Doesn't reach | It covers only cobalt, graphite, lithium and nickel. | Neither rare earths nor tin are in scope. |
| EU Forced Labor Regulation | Not yet in force | Documented forced labor at mines is exactly what this ban targets, from December 2027. | Until then the EU has no tool. Afterwards, authorities still have to prove the link product by product. |
| Tariff Act §307 import ban | Applies | Forced labor at a mine anywhere is enough. Anyone can petition CBP, which can hold goods on reasonable but not conclusive evidence. | The law fits today, but someone has to tie the mine to specific imported goods, and separation in China makes that hard. |
| Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act | Doesn't reach | Xinjiang and listed companies only. | There is no presumption for Myanmar, even with forced labor documented. |
| Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive | Not yet in force | Forced labor and pollution are in scope from July 2029 for very large companies. | Nothing happens before 2029, and most fusion suppliers are too small to be covered. |
| Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (LkSG) | Partly reaches | Risk analysis normally stops at direct suppliers. | A German magnet buyer is not expected to look past its first-tier supplier toward Wa State. |
| Human Rights Due Diligence Guidelines | Voluntary only | Recommendation only. | Japanese tape and wire makers face no legal duty to check for forced labor upstream. |
| OECD Minerals Due Diligence Guidance | Voluntary only | Covers all minerals, all areas. | Correct standard, no obligation. |
| Targeted sanctions | Doesn't reach | None of the US or EU measures reviewed here target Wa State's mineral trade. | The armed group profiting from the mines faces no mineral-specific restriction in these programs. |
| Rare earth export controls | Partly reaches | Rare earth exports from China need a license; tin does not fall under these controls. | Separation in China turns Myanmar ore into licensed Chinese product, which erases where it was mined. |
| Producer-country enforcement | Doesn't reach | Wa State suspended all mining in August 2023 to formalize the sector. | The armed group is the regulator, so formalization serves its revenue rather than workers' rights. |

**Suggested side-by-side:** Tariff Act §307 import ban vs. EU Forced Labor Regulation

## Region: Bangka Belitung, Indonesia

*Tin*

**Route:** Pits & dredges (Bangka Belitung) → State permit (PT Timah) → Smelting (Indonesia) → Nb₃Sn wire (fusion magnets)

**Documented harm**

- **~6,300** child laborers in informal tin mining (ILO survey, 2014). An International Labour Organization survey estimated about 6,300 child laborers aged 5–17 in the province's informal tin mines, most of them aged 15–17 and 93% of them boys, almost all in household-run mines. The survey was carried out in 2014, so the current number is unknown. Sources: [ILO, Sectoral survey of child labour in informal tin mining, Bangka Belitung (Dec 2015)](https://www.ilo.org/publications/sectoral-survey-child-labour-informal-tin-mining-kepulauan-bangka-belitung) · [UK government K4D review, citing the ILO survey](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a5f34feed915d7dfb57d02f/209-213-Child-labour-in-mining.pdf)
- **57** deaths in abandoned tin pits, 2014–2020. Indonesia's Mining Advocacy Network (JATAM) counted 57 people who died after falling into abandoned mining pits in Bangka Belitung between 2014 and 2020, the most of any province. Across Indonesia, most of the 168 people killed in abandoned pits were children. Sources: [Mongabay, citing JATAM (Jun 2021)](https://news.mongabay.com/2021/06/tin-mines-close-in-on-an-indonesian-hamlet-still-clinging-to-nature) · [Eco-Business, citing JATAM](https://www.eco-business.com/ms/news/grim-toll-from-indonesias-abandoned-mines-may-get-even-worse-report/)
- **Rp300T** in state losses from illegal tin mining, 2015–2022. Courts convicted businessman Harvey Moeis and others over illegal tin trading in PT Timah's permit area. The losses were put at about Rp300 trillion (around US$18 billion), much of it environmental damage. His 20-year sentence was upheld on final appeal. Sources: [Jakarta Globe](https://jakartaglobe.id/news/mining-tycoon-harvey-moeis-begins-serving-20-years-in-prison-over-rp-300-trillion-tin-corruption-case) · [Indonesian National Police (Feb 2025)](https://inp.polri.go.id/artikel/harvey-moeis-prison-sentence-increased-to-20-years-in-tin-corruption-case)
- **1,000** illegal tin mines targeted in a September 2025 crackdown. President Prabowo Subianto ordered the police, military and customs to shut the smuggling routes from about 1,000 illegal tin mining sites in Bangka Belitung, starting 1 September 2025. Sources: [Antara, Indonesia's state news agency (Sep 2025)](https://en.antaranews.com/news/383205/govt-calls-for-shutdown-of-1000-illegal-tin-mines-in-bangka-belitung)

**Coverage tally:** 1 applies, 4 partly reaches, 1 not yet in force, 2 voluntary only, 3 doesn't reach, 2 not relevant

| Rule | Verdict | What it says here | What the gap lets through |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dodd-Frank §1502 | Doesn't reach | Tin counts, but Indonesia isn't a covered country. | US companies need not ask whether their tin came from Bangka. |
| EU Conflict Minerals Regulation | Partly reaches | Tin is covered, and high-risk areas can include widespread abuses. Whether Bangka counts depends on each importer's own risk assessment. | If an importer decides Bangka isn't high-risk, nothing more is required. |
| EU Battery Regulation due diligence | Doesn't reach | Tin is not a battery raw material under the law. | Not in scope. |
| EU Forced Labor Regulation | Partly reaches | From December 2027 it bans forced child labor. | Hazardous child labor and deadly pits are not automatically forced labor, so they can fall outside. |
| Tariff Act §307 import ban | Partly reaches | It covers forced or indentured child labor. | Children working in dangerous pits for family income may not meet the forced labor test, so the ban may not apply. |
| Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act | Doesn't reach | Xinjiang only. | Not relevant to Indonesian tin. |
| Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive | Not yet in force | Child labor and environmental damage are in scope from July 2029. | The companies buying magnet wire today face no duty for years, and most are too small to be covered. |
| Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (LkSG) | Partly reaches | Child labor is in scope, but risk analysis normally stops at direct suppliers. | A German wire buyer is not expected to trace its tin back to Bangka's pits. |
| Human Rights Due Diligence Guidelines | Voluntary only | Recommendation only. | No legal duty for Japanese wire makers to check tin origin. |
| OECD Minerals Due Diligence Guidance | Voluntary only | Covers tin and all areas; the tin industry built its programs on it. | Voluntary outside the EU importer stage. |
| Targeted sanctions | Not relevant | No sanctions program applies. | This is a domestic governance problem, not a sanctions one. |
| Rare earth export controls | Not relevant | Tin isn't among China's controlled rare earths. | Not relevant here. |
| Producer-country enforcement | Applies | Courts sentenced businessman Harvey Moeis to 20 years over tin trading in PT Timah's permit area from 2015 to 2022. In September 2025 the president ordered about 1,000 illegal mines shut and their smuggling routes cut. | Illegal ore was sold under a state company's legal permit, so the legal paperwork was itself the laundering step. |

## Region: Rubaya, DR Congo → Rwanda

*Coltan (niobium, tantalum)*

**Route:** Rubaya mines (DRC, held by M23) → Smuggled (into Rwanda) → Refining (UAE or China) → Nb₃Sn · NbTi wire (fusion magnets)

**Documented harm**

- **~15%** of the world's coltan. Reuters reports that Rubaya produces around 15% of the world's coltan, the ore processed into tantalum. Global Witness puts the mines at about 15% of global tantalum supply. Sources: [Reuters (Jan 2026)](https://www.reuters.com/world/africa/more-than-200-killed-coltan-mine-collapse-east-congo-official-says-2026-01-30) · [Global Witness, Who buys Rwanda's smuggled coltan?](https://globalwitness.org/en/campaigns/transition-minerals/who-buys-rwandas-smuggled-coltan-the-global-journey-of-conflict-coltan-from-drc-to-the-worlds-electronics)
- **$800k** a month to M23 from coltan taxes. UN experts found that after seizing Rubaya in April 2024, M23 controlled the trade and transport of about 120 metric tons of coltan a month and collected at least $800,000 a month in taxes. Coltan smuggled to Rwanda and mixed with Rwandan output caused the largest contamination of mineral supply chains recorded in the Great Lakes region. Sources: [UN Group of Experts on the DRC, S/2024/969 (Dec 2024)](https://documents.un.org/doc/undoc/gen/n24/373/37/pdf/n2437337.pdf) · [Global Witness, Who buys Rwanda's smuggled coltan?](https://globalwitness.org/en/campaigns/transition-minerals/who-buys-rwandas-smuggled-coltan-the-global-journey-of-conflict-coltan-from-drc-to-the-worlds-electronics)
- **Forced** labor and forced recruitment documented by Amnesty (2026). Amnesty International documented summary killings, torture, arbitrary detention, forced labor and forced recruitment by M23 at Rubaya and the Lomera gold mine, including 18 miners killed. Miners were forced to work underground for more than a week at a time. Sources: [Amnesty International, 'We didn't come to play' (Sep 2026)](https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/afr62/1454/2026/en) · [Amnesty International news release (Sep 2026)](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/09/drc-m23-fighters-abused-miners-and-trafficked-gold-and-coltan-to-rwanda)
- **200+** killed in each of two Rubaya mine collapses in 2026. Officials reported more than 200 deaths in a collapse at Rubaya in late January 2026, and more than 200 more in a landslide caused by heavy rain in early March 2026. Miners dig by hand for a few dollars a day. Sources: [Reuters (Jan 2026)](https://www.reuters.com/world/africa/more-than-200-killed-coltan-mine-collapse-east-congo-official-says-2026-01-30) · [Reuters (Mar 2026)](https://www.reuters.com/business/environment/landslide-kills-over-200-people-congos-rubaya-mine-mines-ministry-says-2026-03-04)

**Coverage tally:** 2 applies, 3 partly reaches, 2 not yet in force, 2 voluntary only, 3 doesn't reach, 1 not relevant

| Rule | Verdict | What it says here | What the gap lets through |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dodd-Frank §1502 | Partly reaches | Coltan and tin from the DRC and Rwanda are covered, so US-listed companies must ask about origin and file Form SD. | It's disclosure, not a ban; the detailed report has gone unenforced since 2017; and niobium isn't a listed derivative, so a niobium product can slip through. |
| EU Conflict Minerals Regulation | Applies | EU importers of tantalum and tin ores and metals must carry out full due diligence. | Once refined in the UAE or China the metal arrives with a new origin, and finished wire or magnets aren't covered at all. |
| EU Battery Regulation due diligence | Doesn't reach | Covers only cobalt, graphite, lithium and nickel. | Tantalum and niobium are outside the battery rules. |
| EU Forced Labor Regulation | Not yet in force | Forced labor at Rubaya fits squarely, from December 2027. | Until then, the EU has no product ban for this. |
| Tariff Act §307 import ban | Applies | Documented forced labor at the mine is grounds for a petition and a detention order. | Relabeling through Rwanda and refiners abroad makes it hard to prove which imports came from Rubaya. |
| Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act | Doesn't reach | Xinjiang only. | Not relevant here. |
| Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive | Not yet in force | Armed-group financing and forced labor are in scope from July 2029. | Most fusion suppliers are too small to be covered, and nothing applies before 2029. |
| Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (LkSG) | Partly reaches | Risk analysis normally stops at direct suppliers. | A German magnet buyer is not expected to look through Rwandan exporters and foreign refiners. |
| Human Rights Due Diligence Guidelines | Voluntary only | Recommendation only. | Japanese wire and magnet makers face no legal duty here. |
| OECD Minerals Due Diligence Guidance | Voluntary only | The Guidance was written with this region in mind. | Still voluntary for anyone past the EU importer stage. |
| Targeted sanctions | Partly reaches | In August 2025 the US sanctioned PARECO-FF, a Congolese mining cooperative and two Hong Kong buyers. In March 2025 the EU sanctioned M23 leaders, Rwandan army officers and a company. | The 2025 US mining sanctions hit the mine's former controller, not M23, which runs it now. |
| Rare earth export controls | Not relevant | China's rare earth controls don't cover tantalum or niobium. | Not relevant, though Chinese refining is one of the steps that hides origin. |
| Producer-country enforcement | Doesn't reach | M23 runs a parallel administration that taxes and controls mining and transport. | Congolese mining law doesn't operate where the state has lost control. |
