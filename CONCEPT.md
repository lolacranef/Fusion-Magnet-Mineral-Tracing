# Coilchain: Magnet Mineral Rule Gaps

*A stress test of the rules that should stop human rights abuses behind fusion superconducting magnets: for each documented abuse, which rules its route actually passes, how it gets through each one, and who keeps the gap open. Rule terms checked 9 November 2026. Research, not legal advice.*

Interactive site: <https://lolacranef.github.io/Fusion-Magnet-Mineral-Tracing/>. Methods and every source: [DATA_METHODS_SOURCES.md](DATA_METHODS_SOURCES.md).

## Who this is for

### Primary: policy reviewers and public fusion funders
- **Who:** staff reviewing the EU Conflict Minerals Regulation and Japan's due diligence guidelines, officials building the EU forced labor risk database, and program officers who fund fusion through DOE milestone awards, UK STEP and national research ministries.
- **Decision they make:** which minerals and regions a rule covers, and what conditions come with public money.
- **Why now:** the EU regulation is under review in 2026, Japan's review ends in 2026, and the EU forced labor ban starts in December 2027. Fusion buys tiny volumes and much of it is publicly funded, so a sourcing condition costs little and reaches nearly every magnet maker.
- **Use it to:** show, region by region, exactly how a documented abuse gets past each rule, and who keeps that gap open.

### Secondary: investigators, NGOs and journalists
- **Decision:** which tool to use against a documented abuse, such as a §307 allegation to CBP or a sanctions referral.
- **Use it to:** match a region's evidence to the one rule that actually applies today, using the plain-language rule guide and links to the official texts.

### Secondary: compliance and procurement at fusion companies
- **Decision:** what to ask their tape and wire suppliers, beyond what the law requires.
- **Use it to:** see which leaks no law will close for them, and build the questions that close them into supplier contracts.

## The short version

We tested 13 rules against the documented abuses in 4 mining regions. A rule is tested only where the abuse's real route passes it. Of 45 tests, none hold. In 26 the abuse gets through a specific loophole; 3 fit on paper but the proof they need is blocked; 8 are not yet in force; 8 create no legal duty. Governments open or keep open 18 of the 45 failures.

## How to read a test

Each test starts from the documented abuse and follows the mineral along its route: mine, border and paperwork, processing, tape or wire maker. It records what the rule would require there, how the abuse gets through, the leak type, who opens the leak, the step where it happens, and whether that is a documented case or a structural gap the rule's text allows.

### Results

| Result | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Holds | Would stop the abuse today |
| Holds on paper | Fits, but the proof it needs is blocked |
| Not yet in force | Would apply, but only later |
| No legal duty | Recommends; nobody has to comply |
| Leaks | A specific loophole lets the abuse through |
| Not tested | The abuse's route never passes this rule |

### Leak types

| Leak type | What happens | Tests |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed lists | The rule names minerals, countries or companies, and the trade routes around the list. | 6 |
| Mixing | Ore is blended with other supply, so no single shipment can be traced back to the mine. | 1 |
| Laundering paperwork | A legal document, such as a permit, tag or certificate, covers ore it shouldn't. | 4 |
| Certificate accepted as proof | The rule or the buyer accepts an industry audit, and the audit doesn't test this risk. | 2 |
| Third-party buyer | An unlisted company buys from the sanctioned one, and everyone downstream deals only with it. | 1 |
| State-held data | A government records where the ore came from but doesn't share it. | 2 |
| State capture or non-enforcement | Officials issue cover, take part, or don't enforce. | 4 |
| Not knowing is safe | Duties stop at the first supplier unless the buyer already knows of a problem, so not asking is the safe choice. | 5 |
| Size threshold | The companies closest to the risk are too small to be covered. | 4 |
| Wrong harm | The rule only bites on one kind of harm, and the harm here is another kind. | 4 |
| Proof burden | Authorities must tie a mine to specific goods before acting, and the route makes that close to impossible. | 4 |
| No legal duty | The rule only recommends. | 8 |

### Who opens the leak

| Who opens it | Meaning | Tests | Documented |
|---|---|---|---|
| The rule's own design | Scope, lists and thresholds written into the rule | 16 | 7 |
| Governments | Officials who issue cover, keep data secret, or don't enforce | 18 | 11 |
| Companies | Buyers and suppliers that don't ask, or accept paper | 6 | 6 |
| Industry schemes | Audits and tags accepted as proof | 4 | 3 |
| Armed groups | Groups that run the mines and decide what leaves | 1 | 1 |

## The rules

### Disclosure & due diligence laws

#### Dodd-Frank §1502 (US)

A US securities law that makes listed companies say whether their products contain tin, tantalum, tungsten or gold from Congo or its neighbors. It bans nothing: the pressure comes from public disclosure.

- **Minerals covered:** Tin, tantalum, tungsten, gold. Not niobium or rare earths.
- **Where:** DRC and 9 neighbors
- **Who must act:** US-listed companies
- **What it requires:** Country-of-origin inquiry, disclosed on Form SD. No ban.
- **Status:** In force; detailed report unenforced since 2017; future uncertain.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Conflict minerals (3TG):* Cassiterite, coltan, wolframite and gold, and their derivatives tin, tantalum and tungsten. Niobium, which also comes from coltan, is not a listed derivative.
  - *Covered countries:* The DRC and the nine countries that border it.
  - *Reasonable country of origin inquiry:* A good-faith check, usually a supplier survey, on where the minerals came from. Every filer must do one.
  - *Form SD:* The annual 'specialized disclosure' filed with the SEC, due by 31 May each year, or the next business day if that falls on a weekend.
  - *Conflict Minerals Report:* A fuller due diligence report for minerals that may come from covered countries. Since an April 2017 statement by SEC staff, companies that skip it have not faced enforcement.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Search SEC EDGAR for form type SD to see which smelters and refiners a company names, and whether it filed a Conflict Minerals Report.
  - Compare a company's smelter list with the Responsible Minerals Initiative's lists of audited smelters.
- **Where it leaks:**
  - *Kachin: not tested.* Rare earths aren't on its list of minerals, so the abuse's route never meets a filing duty.
  - *Shan & Wa: leaks (fixed lists; opened by the rule's own design).* Myanmar isn't a covered country, so tin from the UWSA-held Man Maw mine produces a clean filing. Reuters found in 2016 that Yunnan Tin, the world's largest producer, bought about a third of its tin from the Wa mine. A solder maker supplying Apple's contractors told Reuters that because Myanmar was not treated as conflict-affected, a smelter's certificate was 'acceptable'.
  - *Bangka Belitung: leaks (fixed lists; opened by the rule's own design).* Indonesia isn't a covered country, so a filer that traces its tin to a Bangka smelter can stop there. Child labor and deadly abandoned pits are not conflict risks under this law.
  - *Rubaya: leaks (laundering paperwork; opened by governments).* Three gaps line up. Niobium, the metal fusion wire needs, isn't a listed derivative. The SEC has not enforced the detailed report since 2017. And coltan smuggled into Rwanda leaves with tags that let filers record it as conflict-free.
- **Official text and tools:** [15 U.S.C. §78m(p): the statute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/78m) · [17 CFR §240.13p-1: the SEC rule](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.13p-1) · [SEC EDGAR filing search](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/) · [Responsible Minerals Initiative smelter lists](https://www.responsiblemineralsinitiative.org/smelters-refiners-lists/)
- **Secondary sources:** [Skadden (May 2026)](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/05/conflict-minerals-disclosures-due-june-1-2026) · [Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/viewpoints/102kbie/is-u-s-conflict-minerals-disclosure-nearing-an-end) · [Claigan FAQ](https://www.claigan.com/conflict-minerals-faq/)

#### EU Conflict Minerals Regulation (EU 2017/821)

An EU regulation that makes companies importing tin, tantalum, tungsten or gold ores and metals into the EU check their supply chains against the OECD Guidance. It covers conflict areas anywhere, but only those four metals and only as ores or metals.

- **Minerals covered:** Tin, tantalum, tungsten, gold: ores and metals only.
- **Where:** Conflict-affected and high-risk areas worldwide
- **Who must act:** EU importers of ores and metals above thresholds
- **What it requires:** OECD-based supply chain due diligence; RMAP audits accepted since October 2025.
- **Status:** Applies since January 2021; under review in 2026.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Union importer:* The company that declares the ores or metals for release into the EU. Importers of finished goods, such as wire or magnets, are not covered.
  - *CAHRA:* Conflict-affected and high-risk areas: places in armed conflict, fragile after conflict, or with weak governance and widespread abuses. The EU publishes an indicative, non-binding list; importers must still judge risk themselves.
  - *Annex I thresholds:* Minimum yearly import volumes. Importers below them have no duties.
  - *Competent authority:* The national body in each EU country that checks importers and can order corrective action. Each country sets its own rules for penalties.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Ask an EU country's competent authority whether a named importer has been checked.
  - Use the CAHRA definition to argue that an area such as Wa State should trigger enhanced checks for tin.
- **Where it leaks:**
  - *Kachin: not tested.* Rare earths aren't covered, even though Kachin meets the regulation's own definition of a conflict-affected area. Tin from the same conflict would need due diligence; yttrium doesn't.
  - *Shan & Wa: leaks (certificate accepted as proof; opened by governments).* Since October 2025 importers buying from smelters audited under RMAP, the industry scheme the EU recognised, can rely on that audit. An audit checks the smelter's process; once Wa ore is mixed with Chinese ore no shipment shows where it came from. Wire makers importing finished Nb₃Sn wire, or tin below the regulation's volume thresholds, have no duty at all.
  - *Bangka Belitung: leaks (laundering paperwork; opened by companies).* The importer decides whether Bangka is high-risk, and the EU's list of such areas is only indicative. The documents an importer would check were the ones corrupted: from 2015 to 2022 illegal ore was sold into PT Timah's legal permit area through private smelters and, prosecutors said, shell companies set up for the purpose.
  - *Rubaya: leaks (certificate accepted as proof; opened by industry schemes).* Importers can rely on refiners audited under RMAP, and those refiners rely on mine tags upstream. Global Witness reports smugglers getting tags for Rubaya coltan from Rwandan officials. ITSCI, which runs the tags, disputes this but has suspended or expelled exporters since 2024. UN experts say Rwanda exports more tantalum than it produces. Finished wire isn't covered at all.
- **Official text and tools:** [Regulation (EU) 2017/821 (EUR-Lex)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/821/oj)
- **Secondary sources:** [Ropes & Gray on the CAHRA list (Dec 2020)](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2020/12/conflict-minerals-compliance-european-commission-publishes-global-list-of-conflict-affected) · [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/the-eu-new-mandatory-supply-chain-diligence-rules-batteries-raw-materials/)

#### Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (EU 2026/470)

An EU directive requiring the largest companies to find and address human rights and environmental harm in their own operations and their chain of activities. A 2026 amendment sharply cut its scope and delayed it to 2029.

- **Minerals covered:** All (harm-based).
- **Where:** Whole chain of activities, worldwide
- **Who must act:** EU companies with 5,000+ staff and over €1.5bn turnover; non-EU companies with over €1.5bn EU turnover
- **What it requires:** Risk-based due diligence.
- **Status:** Comply by 26 July 2029.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Directive:* EU law that each member country must turn into national law ('transposition') before companies are bound by it.
  - *Chain of activities:* A company's upstream suppliers, direct and indirect, and some downstream partners. Mines are included in principle.
  - *Adverse impact:* A harm to people or the environment listed in the directive's annex, including forced and child labor.
  - *Risk-based scoping:* Since the 2026 amendment, companies identify where harm is most likely and severe and look deeper there, instead of mapping every supplier.
  - *Penalties:* Fines capped at 3% of worldwide turnover. The amendment removed the EU-wide civil liability rule.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Check whether a supplier's parent group meets the thresholds: 5,000+ staff and over €1.5bn turnover, or over €1.5bn EU turnover for non-EU companies.
  - From 2029, raise documented harm with the company and with the national supervisory authority.
- **Where it leaks:**
  - *Kachin: not yet in force (size threshold; opened by the rule's own design).* The companies closest to Kachin yttrium, tape makers and fusion developers, are far below 5,000 staff and €1.5bn turnover. Risk-based scoping lets a large buyer stop where its supplier's paperwork stops: at a Chinese separator.
  - *Shan & Wa: not yet in force (size threshold; opened by the rule's own design).* Nothing applies before 2029, and most fusion suppliers are too small to be covered at all.
  - *Bangka Belitung: not yet in force (size threshold; opened by the rule's own design).* The companies buying magnet wire today face no duty for years, and most are too small to be covered at all.
  - *Rubaya: not yet in force (size threshold; opened by the rule's own design).* Nothing applies before 2029, and most fusion suppliers are too small to be covered at all.
- **Official text and tools:** [Directive (EU) 2024/1760, current version (EUR-Lex)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/1760/oj) · [Amending Directive (EU) 2026/470 (EUR-Lex)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2026/470/oj/eng)
- **Secondary sources:** [Covington (Feb 2026)](https://www.cov.com/news-and-insights/insights/2026/02/eu-csddd-csrd-omnibus-published-in-official-journal-transposition-delegated-acts-and-guidelines-are-next)

#### Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (LkSG) (Germany)

A German law requiring companies with 1,000+ staff in Germany to manage human rights and environmental risks in their supply chains. Duties are strongest for their own operations and direct suppliers. It is being cut back.

- **Minerals covered:** All (harm-based).
- **Where:** Global, usually direct suppliers only
- **Who must act:** Companies in Germany with 1,000+ staff
- **What it requires:** Risk analysis, prevention, complaints procedure.
- **Status:** Being cut back; amendment in Bundestag.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Direct supplier:* A company the buyer contracts with directly. Risk analysis is required for these.
  - *Indirect supplier:* Suppliers further up the chain, such as smelters and mines. Duties apply only when the company has 'substantiated knowledge' of a possible violation.
  - *Substantiated knowledge:* Concrete, verifiable information pointing to a possible violation, such as an NGO report or a complaint.
  - *Complaints procedure:* Every covered company must run one, open to anyone, including people affected at indirect suppliers.
  - *BAFA:* The Federal Office for Economic Affairs and Export Control, which enforces the law and accepts complaints.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Send documented evidence about a mine to a German buyer's complaints procedure. This can create 'substantiated knowledge' and trigger duties toward indirect suppliers.
  - Complain to BAFA if the company does not respond.
- **Where it leaks:**
  - *Kachin: leaks (not knowing is safe; opened by the rule's own design).* Global Witness has publicly tied Myanmar rare earths to Chinese state-owned processors since 2022. But a tape buyer that never asks which separator its supplier uses never holds knowledge about its own chain, so the duty to look past the first supplier is never triggered.
  - *Shan & Wa: leaks (not knowing is safe; opened by the rule's own design).* A German magnet or wire buyer checks its wire maker. Wa tin sits three tiers further up, mixed in a Chinese smelter, so the buyer never holds the knowledge that would oblige it to look, unless someone files a complaint.
  - *Bangka Belitung: leaks (not knowing is safe; opened by the rule's own design).* A German wire buyer checks its wire maker, not Bangka's pits. Without a complaint it never holds the knowledge that would oblige it to look further.
  - *Rubaya: leaks (not knowing is safe; opened by the rule's own design).* A German magnet buyer is not expected to look through Rwandan exporters and foreign refiners. The tag on the bag gives it a reason not to.
- **Official text and tools:** [LkSG, official text in German](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/lksg/) · [BAFA: overview in English](https://www.bafa.de/EN/Supply_Chain_Act/Overview/overview_node.html) · [BAFA: complaints procedure](https://www.bafa.de/EN/Supply_Chain_Act/Complaints_Procedure/complaints_procedure_node.html)
- **Secondary sources:** [Taylor Wessing (Jan 2026)](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2026/01/lksg)

### Voluntary standards & industry schemes

#### OECD Minerals Due Diligence Guidance (International)

The international reference standard for responsible mineral sourcing, adopted by OECD governments. It applies to all minerals from all conflict-affected and high-risk areas, but is voluntary unless a law adopts it.

- **Minerals covered:** All minerals.
- **Where:** All areas
- **Who must act:** Any company in a mineral supply chain
- **What it requires:** Five-step framework.
- **Status:** Voluntary reference standard.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Five-step framework:* 1. Build management systems. 2. Identify and assess risks. 3. Respond to them. 4. Have smelters and refiners independently audited. 5. Report publicly every year.
  - *Annex II risks:* Risks a company must never contribute to: torture, forced labor, the worst forms of child labor and war crimes; support to armed groups; abusive security forces; bribery and money laundering.
  - *Upstream and downstream:* Upstream runs from the mine to the smelter or refiner; downstream from the smelter to the final product. Smelters are the 'choke point' where origin can still be checked.
  - *Third edition (2016):* Clarified that the Guidance covers all minerals, not only tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Use Annex II as a checklist when reporting on a mine: each documented abuse maps onto a named risk.
  - Complaints about multinational companies can go to OECD National Contact Points under the related OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises.
- **Where it leaks:**
  - *Kachin: no legal duty (no legal duty; opened by companies).* Global Witness traced Myanmar rare earths through Chinese processors to magnet makers supplying major brands, and named 78 companies. Most did not or would not say where their rare earths came from. Nothing obliged them to.
  - *Shan & Wa: no legal duty (no legal duty; opened by companies).* Tin revenue is a major source of income for the UWSA's territory. The Guidance's answer is to stop buying, but no law makes a tin buyer apply it beyond the EU importer stage.
  - *Bangka Belitung: no legal duty (no legal duty; opened by companies).* Illegal ore entered a state company's legal supply for seven years without the industry's OECD-based programs flagging it. Voluntary checks read the same paperwork the scheme corrupted.
  - *Rubaya: no legal duty (no legal duty; opened by companies).* The laundering happens inside the system the Guidance created: tags and audits that look like compliance. Outside the EU importer stage nobody is legally required to look behind them.
- **Official text and tools:** [OECD Guidance: official page](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-due-diligence-guidance-for-responsible-supply-chains-of-minerals-from-conflict-affected-and-high-risk-areas_9789264252479-en.html) · [Full text (PDF)](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2016/04/oecd-due-diligence-guidance-for-responsible-supply-chains-of-minerals-from-conflict-affected-and-high-risk-areas_g1g65996/9789264252479-en.pdf)
- **Secondary sources:** [International Tin Association](https://www.internationaltin.org/oecd/)

#### Human Rights Due Diligence Guidelines (Japan)

Japanese government guidelines, published in September 2022, that ask companies doing business in Japan to carry out human rights due diligence across their supply chains. They are voluntary.

- **Minerals covered:** All.
- **Where:** Global value chains
- **Who must act:** Companies operating in Japan
- **What it requires:** UNGP-aligned due diligence, recommended.
- **Status:** Voluntary; review ends 2026.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Human rights due diligence:* A repeated process to identify, prevent and reduce harm to people, track the results and report on them.
  - *UN Guiding Principles:* The 2011 UN framework stating that companies have a responsibility to respect human rights. The guidelines are based on it.
  - *Grievance mechanism:* A channel through which affected people can raise concerns with a company.
  - *Voluntary:* There is no penalty for ignoring the guidelines. A government review is due by the end of 2026, with any bill in 2027.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Ask Japanese tape and wire makers whether they apply the guidelines to rare earths and tin, and to name their separators and smelters.
- **Where it leaks:**
  - *Kachin: no legal duty (no legal duty; opened by governments).* Japan hosts the largest REBCO tape makers. Nothing makes them name their separator or ask whether their yttrium came from Kachin, and the government's 2026 review has not yet produced a bill.
  - *Shan & Wa: no legal duty (no legal duty; opened by governments).* Japanese tape and wire makers face no legal duty to ask whether their tin or rare earths passed through Wa-held mines.
  - *Bangka Belitung: no legal duty (no legal duty; opened by governments).* Japanese wire makers face no duty to check whether their tin came from a licensed mine or an illegal pit.
  - *Rubaya: no legal duty (no legal duty; opened by governments).* Japanese wire and magnet makers face no legal duty to ask whether their niobium passed through Rwanda.
- **Official text and tools:** [Guidelines, provisional English translation (METI)](https://www.meti.go.jp/policy/economy/business-jinken/guidelines/provisionalenglishtranslation.pdf) · [METI: business and human rights](https://www.meti.go.jp/english/policy/economy/biz_human_rights/index.html)
- **Secondary sources:** [METI: business and human rights](https://www.meti.go.jp/english/policy/economy/biz_human_rights/index.html)

#### Industry schemes and company codes (Private)

The private systems most laws lean on: smelter audits under the Responsible Minerals Assurance Process (RMAP), mine-to-export tagging under ITSCI in central Africa, and buyers' own supplier codes. They are voluntary, but since October 2025 the EU accepts RMAP audits as meeting its conflict minerals rules.

- **Minerals covered:** Mostly tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold.
- **Where:** Wherever members buy
- **Who must act:** Smelters, exporters and the brands that rely on them
- **What it requires:** Smelter audits (RMAP), mine tagging (ITSCI), supplier codes of conduct.
- **Status:** Voluntary, but the EU has accepted RMAP audits as compliance since October 2025.
- **Key terms:**
  - *RMAP:* The Responsible Minerals Initiative's audit of smelters and refiners. A 'conformant' smelter has passed an audit of its sourcing process, not a check of every shipment.
  - *ITSCI:* A tagging scheme for tin, tantalum and tungsten in central Africa. Bags are tagged at the mine and the tags follow the mineral to export.
  - *Recognition:* Under Implementing Decision (EU) 2025/2071, the EU treats importers that effectively use RMAP as meeting the regulation's requirements.
  - *Supplier code of conduct:* A buyer's contract terms for its direct suppliers. Most stop at the first tier and accept a smelter certificate as evidence.
  - *Mixing:* Smelters blend ore from many sources. Unless feed is tracked by mine, an audit can't show which shipments contained high-risk ore.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Check whether a smelter named in a company's filing is RMAP-conformant, then ask what share of its feed came from Myanmar, Rwanda or informal mines.
  - Compare an exporter's tagged volumes with known production. Global Witness and UN experts use this to spot laundering.
- **Where it leaks:**
  - *Kachin: leaks (not knowing is safe; opened by companies).* In 2024 Global Witness found China's heavy rare earth quota held by two state-owned groups, one formed from companies that had disclosed buying Myanmar heavy rare earths. Brands' supplier codes stop at the magnet or tape maker, so a purchase made through these groups looks clean on paper.
  - *Shan & Wa: leaks (mixing; opened by industry schemes).* Yunnan Tin mixed Wa ore with Chinese ore, so it could not say which shipments contained it (Reuters, 2016). Global Witness said then that the main audit scheme had focused only on central Africa. Downstream buyers accepted the smelter's certificate as proof.
  - *Bangka Belitung: leaks (laundering paperwork; opened by industry schemes).* An audit reads permits and purchase records. In the Timah case the permits were the cover: ore from illegal miners was bought by private smelters and booked as PT Timah's own. A paper audit has nothing to catch.
  - *Rubaya: leaks (laundering paperwork; opened by industry schemes).* Global Witness reported in 2022 and again in 2026 that tags are used to launder smuggled minerals; one smuggler said Rwandan officials bring as many tags as he needs. ITSCI rejects the claim about officials and has suspended or expelled exporters. Either way, the tag is what turns M23-taxed coltan into 'Rwandan' coltan.
- **Official text and tools:** [RMI: smelter and refiner lists](https://www.responsiblemineralsinitiative.org/smelters-refiners-lists/) · [ITSCI](https://www.itsci.org/) · [Implementing Decision (EU) 2025/2071 (EUR-Lex)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dec_impl/2025/2071/oj/eng)
- **Secondary sources:** [Global Witness, The ITSCI laundromat (Apr 2022)](https://globalwitness.org/en/campaigns/transition-minerals/the-itsci-laundromat/) · [ITSCI response to Global Witness (Jun 2026)](https://www.itsci.org/2026/06/12/itsci-response-to-global-witness-report-on-rwandas-coltan/) · [European Commission on RMAP recognition (Oct 2025)](https://policy.trade.ec.europa.eu/news/first-supply-chain-due-diligence-scheme-recognised-under-conflict-minerals-regulation-facilitate-2025-10-17_en) · [Reuters via SDCExec (Nov 2016)](https://www.sdcexec.com/risk-compliance/news/12283021/for-apple-and-others-tin-supply-chain-has-ties-to-rebelheld-myanmar-mine)

### Border bans

#### Tariff Act §307 import ban (US)

A 1930 US trade law that bans imports of goods made wholly or partly by forced, convict or indentured labor, including forced child labor, from any country. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) enforces it.

- **Minerals covered:** Any forced-labor goods.
- **Where:** Any country
- **Who must act:** US importers; anyone can petition CBP
- **What it requires:** CBP can detain on reasonable but not conclusive evidence.
- **Status:** In force.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Withhold Release Order (WRO):* CBP can hold shipments at the border when information reasonably, but not conclusively, indicates forced labor.
  - *Finding:* A stronger CBP determination that goods are made with forced labor, which allows them to be seized.
  - *Allegation:* Any person outside CBP who has reason to believe goods are made with forced labor may tell CBP. NGOs and journalists regularly do.
  - *Consumptive demand exception:* An old loophole that let in forced-labor goods if US demand could not otherwise be met. Congress removed it in 2016.
  - *Importer's response:* When goods are held, the importer has three months to show it made every reasonable effort to trace the labor.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Submit an allegation through CBP's e-Allegations portal, naming the product, the producer and the evidence.
  - Check CBP's public list of Withhold Release Orders and Findings for past cases and precedents.
- **Where it leaks:**
  - *Kachin: leaks (wrong harm; opened by the rule's own design).* The harm documented in Kachin is toxic pollution, illegal mining and armed-group revenue. None of it is forced labor, so the US's only worldwide import ban has nothing to act on.
  - *Shan & Wa: holds on paper (proof burden; opened by governments).* Trafficked people were documented forced to dig at a Chinese-operated rare earth mine in 2026. To act, CBP must tie that mine to specific imports, and separation in China mixes the ore with other feed. CBP has acted region-wide on a mined product before: artisanal gold from the DRC in 2019.
  - *Bangka Belitung: leaks (wrong harm; opened by the rule's own design).* An ILO survey found about 6,300 child laborers in Bangka's informal tin mines, mostly in household pits. Hazardous child labor is not automatically forced labor, so it can fall outside the ban entirely.
  - *Rubaya: holds on paper (proof burden; opened by governments).* CBP has acted region-wide on a DRC mined product before, holding artisanal gold in 2019. For coltan, mixing in Rwanda and refining abroad make it hard to link Rubaya to specific imports.

#### EU Forced Labor Regulation (EU 2024/3015)

An EU regulation that will ban any product made wholly or partly with forced labor from being sold in, or exported from, the EU. It covers every stage, including the mine, but only forced labor, not pollution or conflict financing.

- **Minerals covered:** All products.
- **Where:** Any origin, including extraction
- **Who must act:** Anyone placing products on the EU market
- **What it requires:** Ban on products made with forced labor.
- **Status:** Applies from 14 December 2027.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Forced labor:* As defined in ILO Convention No. 29: work extracted under threat of a penalty that a person has not offered voluntarily. Forced child labor is included.
  - *Lead competent authority:* The European Commission handles cases about risks outside the EU; national authorities handle cases inside it.
  - *Risk-based approach:* Authorities focus on the products, sectors and areas with the highest risk, using a public database the Commission is building.
  - *Burden of proof:* Authorities must establish that forced labor was used, but a company's refusal to cooperate can count against it.
  - *Ban decision:* Orders the product off the EU market and blocked at the border, and requires it to be donated, recycled or destroyed.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Once the EU's submission portal opens, anyone can send evidence about a product or area.
  - Detailed first-hand testimony, like the Shan Human Rights Foundation's, is the kind of evidence the regulation is built to use.
- **Where it leaks:**
  - *Kachin: not yet in force (wrong harm; opened by the rule's own design).* Same limit as the US ban: pollution and armed-group revenue are not forced labor, so the ban will have nothing to act on even after 2027.
  - *Shan & Wa: not yet in force (proof burden; opened by the rule's own design).* Until then the EU has no tool. Afterwards authorities must prove the link product by product, and mixing in China is the same obstacle CBP faces.
  - *Bangka Belitung: not yet in force (wrong harm; opened by the rule's own design).* The same gap as the US ban: children working dangerous pits for family income may not meet the forced labor test.
  - *Rubaya: not yet in force (proof burden; opened by the rule's own design).* Until then the EU has no product ban. Afterwards, mixing in Rwanda creates the same proof problem the US faces.
- **Official text and tools:** [Regulation (EU) 2024/3015 (EUR-Lex)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/3015/oj)
- **Secondary sources:** [Mayer Brown (Jun 2026)](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/the-eu-forced-labour-regulation-10-questions-every-general-counsel-should-be-asking)

#### Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (US)

A 2021 US law that presumes goods from China's Xinjiang region, or from companies on a government list, are made with forced labor. Importers must prove otherwise, which reverses the usual burden of proof.

- **Minerals covered:** Any goods.
- **Where:** Xinjiang and Entity List companies
- **Who must act:** US importers
- **What it requires:** Presumption of forced labor; importer must rebut.
- **Status:** In force; 187 listed entities after Aug 2026.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Rebuttable presumption:* Goods are assumed to be made with forced labor unless the importer proves they are not.
  - *Clear and convincing evidence:* A high standard of proof, above 'more likely than not', that importers must meet to overturn the presumption.
  - *UFLPA Entity List:* Companies named by an interagency task force led by the Department of Homeland Security. Their goods fall under the presumption wherever they are made. 187 entities after August 2026.
  - *Priority sectors:* Sectors flagged for enforcement, including aluminum, copper, lithium and steel. Rare earths are not named.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Check the DHS UFLPA Entity List for any processor or trader in a supply chain.
  - Evidence that Myanmar ore is processed by a listed company would bring it under the presumption.
- **Where it leaks:**
  - *Kachin: not tested.* The documented harm in Kachin is pollution and armed-group revenue, not forced labor, and the ore is not processed in Xinjiang.
  - *Shan & Wa: leaks (fixed lists; opened by governments).* The forced labor is in Myanmar and the ore is processed in China outside Xinjiang. Unless DHS lists the Chinese mine operator or the processor, the presumption never applies, even with forced labor documented.
  - *Bangka Belitung: not tested.* Bangka tin is smelted in Indonesia. No step passes through Xinjiang or a listed company, and the documented harm is not forced labor.
  - *Rubaya: leaks (fixed lists; opened by governments).* Coltan refined in China outside Xinjiang carries no presumption unless DHS lists the refiner, even though forced labor at the mine is documented.
- **Official text and tools:** [Public Law 117-78 (congress.gov)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/6256) · [DHS: UFLPA Entity List](https://www.dhs.gov/uflpa-entity-list) · [CBP: UFLPA guidance](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/forced-labor/UFLPA)
- **Secondary sources:** [Troutman (Aug 2026)](https://www.troutman.com/insights/dhs-adds-43-companies-to-uflpa-entity-list-in-largest-ever-single-expansion-effective-august-3/)

### Sanctions & state controls

#### Targeted sanctions (US / EU)

US and EU sanctions freeze the assets of named people and companies and bar others from dealing with them. They target who is involved, not which mineral is traded.

- **Minerals covered:** Not mineral-based.
- **Where:** Country and counter-narcotics programs
- **Who must act:** Anyone dealing with listed parties
- **What it requires:** No dealings; assets frozen.
- **Status:** Varies by region.
- **Key terms:**
  - *SDN list:* The US Treasury's Specially Designated Nationals list. US persons may not deal with anyone on it.
  - *Asset freeze:* Funds and property of a listed party are blocked.
  - *50% rule:* Under US rules, a company owned 50% or more by listed parties is treated as blocked even if it is not named.
  - *Travel ban:* EU measures can also bar listed people from entering the EU.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - Search OFAC's Sanctions List Search and the EU Sanctions Map for any trader, exporter or refiner named in reporting.
  - Documented links between a buyer and a listed armed group can support a referral to US or EU authorities.
- **Where it leaks:**
  - *Kachin: leaks (fixed lists; opened by governments).* Since October 2024 the main mines have been held by the Kachin Independence Army, which none of the measures reviewed here list. In July 2025 the US removed four junta-linked parties from its list. No measure targets the rare earth trade itself, so the money moves without touching a listed name.
  - *Shan & Wa: leaks (third-party buyer; opened by governments).* Once a Chinese smelter buys and mixes the ore, the tin is the smelter's property, not the UWSA's, and US buyers deal only with the smelter. Sanctions experts told Reuters in 2016 that even indirect use might breach US law. The trade went on: the Wa mine resumed exports to China in 2025.
  - *Bangka Belitung: not tested.* No armed group or sanctioned party runs this trade. The harm comes from corporate crime inside a legal permit system.
  - *Rubaya: leaks (fixed lists; opened by governments).* The US mining sanctions hit the mine's former controller, not M23, which runs it now. Sanctions follow named parties, and the coltan leaves through Rwandan exporters: Global Witness found seven companies handled almost 85% of Rwanda's coltan exports from 2023 to 2025.
- **Official text and tools:** [OFAC: DRC-related sanctions](https://ofac.treasury.gov/sanctions-programs-and-country-information/democratic-republic-of-the-congo-related-sanctions) · [OFAC Sanctions List Search](https://sanctionssearch.ofac.treas.gov/) · [EU Sanctions Map](https://www.sanctionsmap.eu/)
- **Secondary sources:** [OFAC: UWSA Kingpin Act designation (Jun 2003)](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20030602) · [The Star (Apr 2026)](https://www.thestar.com.my/news/world/2026/04/27/eu-extends-sanctions-against-myanmar-by-a-year) · [ThePrint (Jul 2025)](https://theprint.in/world/us-lifts-some-myanmar-sanctions-after-general-asked-trump-for-relief/2702840) · [Mining.com (Aug 2025)](https://www.mining.com/web/us-sanctions-armed-group-mining-firms-over-illicit-congo-minerals) · [AllAfrica / Global Witness (Apr 2025)](https://allafrica.com/stories/202504280598.html)

#### China's rare earth controls (China)

Chinese rules that license every export of seven medium and heavy rare earths, including yttrium and gadolinium, and since 2024–25 require miners, separators and exporters to log every rare earth flow, including imported ore, in a state traceability system. The aim is strategic control, not human rights, and none of the data is public.

- **Minerals covered:** Seven medium and heavy rare earths for export licenses, including yttrium and gadolinium; all rare earths for traceability.
- **Where:** Mining, separation and exports in China, including imported ore
- **Who must act:** Chinese miners, separators and exporters
- **What it requires:** Per-shipment export license, and flow records in a state traceability system. Neither is public.
- **Status:** Export controls enforced since April 2025; traceability since October 2024, extended to imports in August 2025.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Announcement No. 18 (April 2025):* Added samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium, and their compounds, alloys and magnets, to China's dual-use export control list. Still in force.
  - *Rare Earth Management Regulations (October 2024):* Set up a national traceability system: companies that mine, smelt, separate or export rare earths must record their product flows in it.
  - *Interim measures (August 2025):* Extended quotas and traceability to imported ore, such as heavy rare earth clays from Myanmar, with monthly reporting to a government platform.
  - *Dual-use item:* Goods with both civilian and military uses, which need a license to export under China's Export Control Law.
  - *Announcement No. 61 (October 2025):* Extended controls to products made outside China containing as little as 0.1% Chinese-origin rare earths by value. China suspended it on 7 November 2025 until 10 November 2026. Check whether the suspension has been extended.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - License and traceability data are not public, so these rules hide origin from buyers while the state records it. Ask suppliers for the separator's name, its export license reference and the origin of its feed.
- **Where it leaks:**
  - *Kachin: leaks (state-held data; opened by governments).* The Chinese state sees Myanmar feed arrive, in customs data and in the new traceability platform, but publishes neither the license data nor the traceability records. The yttrium leaves as licensed Chinese product, and the record of where it was dug stays with the government.
  - *Shan & Wa: leaks (state-held data; opened by governments).* As in Kachin, the state records imported feed but publishes nothing, and the product leaves as Chinese. For tin there isn't even a license: Wa concentrate enters Yunnan's smelters and leaves as Chinese metal.
  - *Bangka Belitung: not tested.* Tin isn't among China's controlled rare earths, and the route doesn't pass through China.
  - *Rubaya: not tested.* China's rare earth controls don't cover tantalum or niobium.
- **Official text and tools:** [IEA: Regulations on the Management of Rare Earths](https://www.iea.org/policies/25387-regulations-on-the-management-of-rare-earths) · [IEA summary of Announcement No. 18](https://www.iea.org/policies/26796-export-controls-on-certain-medium-and-heavy-rare-earth-items) · [CSET translation of Announcement No. 61](https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/mofcom-notice-2025-61) · [Pillsbury: the November 2025 suspension](https://www.pillsburylaw.com/en/news-and-insights/china-suspends-export-controls-certain-critical-minerals-related-items.html)
- **Secondary sources:** [Holland & Knight on Announcement No. 18 (Apr 2025)](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/04/china-imposes-export-controls-on-medium-and-heavy-rare-earth-materials) · [Pillsbury on the November 2025 suspension](https://www.pillsburylaw.com/en/news-and-insights/china-suspends-export-controls-certain-critical-minerals-related-items.html) · [Faegre Drinker on the October 2024 regulations](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2024/10/china-issues-regulations-on-rare-earth-administration) · [Global Times via GlobalSecurity on imported ore (Aug 2025)](https://www.globalsecurity.org/wmd/library/news/china/2025/08/china-250822-globaltimes01.htm)

#### Producer and transit country controls (Local)

The laws, permits and certificates of the countries where ore is mined and the ones it passes through. On paper they ban most of the documented harm. In practice they work only where the state is in control and chooses to enforce, and a transit state can turn smuggled ore into certified exports.

- **Minerals covered:** Whatever is mined or traded there.
- **Where:** Mining and transit countries
- **Who must act:** Miners, traders, officials
- **What it requires:** Mining law, export permits, origin certificates.
- **Status:** Varies; weakest where armed groups or captured agencies run the trade.
- **Key terms:**
  - *Myanmar:* Foreign investment in small and medium-scale mineral production is illegal and no exemptions have been issued, yet mining continues under armed-group control: the KIA in Kachin, the UWSA in Wa State. Myanmar was suspended from the EITI after the 2021 coup.
  - *Indonesia:* Courts convicted those behind illegal tin trading, and a 2025 crackdown targeted 1,000 illegal mines. The seized smelters were handed to PT Timah, the state company whose permits were abused.
  - *DR Congo:* State law does not operate in M23-held Rubaya, where the group runs a parallel administration that taxes minerals.
  - *Rwanda:* Certifies mineral exports as Rwandan. UN experts say its authorities inflate domestic production to hide smuggled coltan; Rwanda denies backing M23.
  - *EITI:* The Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative: a global standard under which member governments publish mining licenses, payments and ownership data.
- **How investigators can use it:**
  - EITI country pages and reports show who holds mining licenses and what they paid.
  - Compare a transit country's exports with its own production in UN Comtrade. A gap points to smuggled supply.
  - Court records in Indonesia's tin case name the companies and smelters involved.
- **Where it leaks:**
  - *Kachin: leaks (state capture or non-enforcement; opened by armed groups).* The state lost control after the 2021 coup. The KIA now decides when ore may leave: in March 2025 it let stockpiles ship to China. Myanmar is suspended from the EITI, so licenses and payments go unpublished.
  - *Shan & Wa: leaks (state capture or non-enforcement; opened by governments).* The UWSA is the regulator. It suspended mining in August 2023 to 'formalize' the sector and reopened it in 2025 to export to China. Testimony published in 2026 says regime police handed trafficked people to UWSA soldiers to work a mine: the state took part instead of acting as a check.
  - *Bangka Belitung: holds on paper (state capture or non-enforcement; opened by governments).* Courts convicted those behind the scheme, one with a 20-year sentence. But prosecutors let seized smelters keep operating, and in October 2025 the state handed them to PT Timah, the state company whose permits were abused. Abandoned pits keep killing people.
  - *Rubaya: leaks (state capture or non-enforcement; opened by governments).* Congolese law doesn't operate in M23-held Rubaya. Across the border, UN experts say Rwandan authorities inflate domestic production figures to hide smuggled exports, and Global Witness found no confiscation of smuggled coltan in two years. The EU signed a raw materials partnership with Rwanda in 2024; the European Parliament called for its suspension in 2025.
- **Official text and tools:** [EITI: Myanmar](https://eiti.org/countries/myanmar) · [EITI: Indonesia](https://eiti.org/countries/indonesia) · [EITI: DR Congo](https://eiti.org/countries/democratic-republic-congo) · [UN Comtrade](https://comtradeplus.un.org/)
- **Secondary sources:** [Mongabay on Global Witness](https://news.mongabay.com/2022/08/toxic-rare-earth-mines-fuel-deforestation-rights-abuses-in-myanmar-report-says/) · [The Irrawaddy on Wa tin](https://www.irrawaddy.com/?p=127037) · [VOI (Jul 2025)](https://voi.id/en/amp/491796) · [Antara (Sep 2025)](https://en.antaranews.com/news/383205/govt-calls-for-shutdown-of-1000-illegal-tin-mines-in-bangka-belitung) · [Reuters via Monitor](https://www.monitor.co.ug/uganda/news/inside-the-mine-that-feeds-the-tech-world-and-funds-congo-s-rebels-5154474) · [Swissinfo on the UN Group of Experts (Jul 2025)](https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/international-geneva/un-experts-warn-congos-conflict-minerals-slipping-into-global-market/89978793) · [Mining.com: seized smelters handed to PT Timah (Oct 2025)](https://www.mining.com/web/indonesia-hands-over-six-seized-tin-smelters-to-state-miner)
